# What is a Lie?

# Teaser

What is a lie? Obviously, when you say something untrue to deceive someone, that’s a lie. But what about other things, like technicalities, omissions, and hyperbole? That’s what we’re going to explore in today’s video!

# Topic

Everyone knows what a lie is, and that you shouldn’t do it, but there’s more to it than that. There’s things that many people don’t consider lies that are, and many things that seem like lies, but actually aren’t. Let’s look at some scenarios.

>   Oopoo: Hey Deg, is it raining outside?

>   teDegano: No, it’s sunny.

>   Oopoo: \*goes outside\*

>   Oopoo: \*comes back in, all wet\*

>   Oopoo: You lied to me!

>   teDegano: …

In this situation, teDegano told Oopoo that it wasn’t raining, but it was. Did teDegano lie?

The obvious answer is that yes, teDegano did lie, because he said something that was untrue. But it’s more complicated than that. Let’s consider several possibilities.

1.  teDegano knew it was raining but said it was sunny. In this case, he lied.
2.  It was sunny when teDegano talked to Oopoo, but by the time Oopoo got outside, it was raining. In this case, teDegano obviously didn’t lie, because what he said was true at that time. But for the rest of the possibilities, let’s assume that it was raining when teDegano talked to Oopoo.
3.  It was sunny last time teDegano checked, and as far as he knew, it was still sunny. Here, teDegano didn’t lie, he simply had the wrong information. He tried to tell Oopoo the truth, but he was wrong, and didn’t lie.

So wait, you can say something that’s false, without lying? Yes. As long as you say what you believe to be true, with the intention of conveying the truth, it’s not a lie. Conversely, if you say what you believe to be false, with the intention of deceiving someone, that’s a lie.

If teDegano had said that it’s raining with the intention of deceiving Oopoo, it wouldn’t matter if it’s actually raining or not, he lied because he was trying to deceive. It’s ok to be wrong about something, that’s not lying, but it’s also important to admit when you’re wrong and correct your factual errors when they happen.

This also applies if you try to tell the truth and lie at the same time. Usually it’s a statement that can has multiple interpretations, and one of them is true, but it’s told in such a way as to intend a different interpretation, which isn’t true. Here’s another scenario:

Bartelix: Cerima, did you drink all the soda pop again?

Cerima: I just drank one can.

Bartelix: Ok.

Cerima: *phew! I hope he never finds out I drank all the cans today!*

Bartelix: Hey! I see all those empty cans next to your chair! You said you only had one! You lied!

Cerima: No I didn’t! I said I “just” drank one, as in, “just now”!

What does Cerima mean by “I just drank one can.”? It’s ambiguous. The way she said it, it makes it sound like she *only* had one can, but it could also mean that she had recently finished drinking a can. When Bartelix calls her out on it, she retreats to the other interpretation, claiming that she didn’t lie.

But, it doesn’t matter how many interpretations there are of what you say, nor if any of them are actually true. What matters is the interpretation you intend other people to take your words as, and whether that is true. Or rather, whether you say what you say with the intention of deceiving them or with the intention of being honest. Cerima was trying to deceive Bartelix, so she was lying.

## Rapidfire examples

So now we know that being wrong about something isn’t lying, and trying to deceive others is lying, regardless of whether or not what you say is actually true. What about other cases? Let’s rapid fire through some other lying-adjacent things:

-   Sarcasm: Sarcasm is usually said with the intention to be funny or ironic, and not with the intention of deceiving someone. So no, sarcasm is not lying.
-   Exaggeration and Hyperbole: This is meant to add extra weight to something to emphasize it. Whether or not it’s a lie depends on whether the over-the-topness is intended to be taken literally or taken with a grain of salt. If it’s meant to be taken literally, then it’s a lie. If it’s just meant to add flavor, then it’s not a lie, because the other person is expected to understand that reality is different from what is said.
-   Fiction: I’ve heard it said that writing fiction is like elaborate lying. I see the comparison, but it’s not lying, because fiction is generally understood to not be real. Tho I have seen some fiction that tries to blur the line and convince that its reality is actual reality, and then that *would* be a lie.
-   Rounding the truth: Sometimes in order to get a point across easily, you have to say something that’s not true. For example, when I lived with my sister, I’d say “my cat likes to sit on my shoulder” when talking to strangers. Because saying “my cat” gets the point across more easily than saying “the cat I live with who actually belongs to my sister”. Because, for the story, who owns the cat isn’t actually that important, and “rounding the truth” to say just “my cat” makes the story easier to communicate. Now, if the other person asked if that was really my cat, then it would be important whether or not it was actually my cat, and then of course I would say “no, actually, it was my sister’s cat”. Rounding the truth to make communication easier is ok as long as it isn’t done with the intention of trying to deceive someone.
-   Intentionally ambiguous language: saying something that’s intentionally ambiguous, hoping the person understands it the wrong way, while having the other meaning as a backup, is lying. That’s what Cerima did.
-   Jokes: Jokes aren’t lies. People aren’t expected to believe there’s an actual chicken crossing the road, nor are they expected to believe there’s an actual butcher out there introducing their wife as “Meet Patty”. It’s intended for humor, not deceit. On the contrary, most good jokes have an element of truth in them, at least inside jokes do.
-   “Hi, how are you? I’m fine.” We’ve all done this greeting exchange, and said things like “good” or “fine” when we’re actually miserable or having a bad day. But is this a lie? Most of the time, we’re not trying to deceive, we’re just trying to be polite. This is usually the start of a conversation, and is more of a formality so two people acknowledge that they’re talking to each other. “Establishing a socket connection” for you computer nerds out there. It can be a lie if you say “fine” with the intention of avoiding talking about your problems, especially with people who care about you who genuinely want to know how you’re doing.
-   Intentional silence: What if someone else says something untrue that you know is false? Like what if your buddy comes in and says in front of your wife that you’re doing great at work, but in reality you’re being let go? In this case, letting the lie pass as truth is also lying, especially when it’s something you’re expected to know about. So yes, you can lie without saying anything.
-   Estimation: If you said “there’s 100 trees” but there’s only 97 trees, it’s not lying, as long as you’re not deceiving intentionally. You’re saying your best guess as to what you think the truth is. And that is not a lie.

## Wordless Lies

There’s also some things that are lies, without words. Yes, you can lie without using words. And I’m not talking about ASL.

If you nod or shake your head with the intention of deceiving someone, that’s a lie. Using any form of body language to deceive is a lie.

If you write something with the intention to deceive, that’s a lie. Writing follows the same rules as spoken language, when it comes to lying. However, it is a bit trickier because a lot of the time, sarcasm doesn’t translate well in text, because it doesn’t have inflections like spoken speech. So be extra careful to not give people the wrong idea when communicating via written words.

If you’re trying to cross the road, but a car is coming, so you pretend to be on your phone until it passes, that’s a lie. By stopping and looking at your phone, you’re telling the car “I’m not trying to cross” when you actually are. You’re deceiving them, so that is a lie. If you intend on letting them go first, wave them on.

If you pretend to be asleep, that’s a lie.

Forging someone’s signature, obviously a lie.

## Promises

Promises are a whole other topic we’ll talk about in another video, but for now we’ll talk about its intersection with lies.

If you make a promise and keep it, you obviously didn’t lie. But what if you make a promise and don’t keep it? Did you lie when you made the promise?

Again, it comes down to intention. If you made the promise with the intention of keeping it, but for whatever reason did not end up keeping it, you did NOT lie when you made the promise (it’s still a broken promise, but that’s for another video). If you made the promise with the intention of forgetting about it, then you DID lie when you made the promise. So it comes down to intention.

## Why it matters

So why does the truth matter? And why do lies hurt?

When we talk to each other, we trust that we’re telling the truth. But when we lie and deceive, we erode trust between each other. Afterall, how can you trust someone to tell the truth when they lie to you all the time about everything? Lies erode trust, and when trust is eroded, relationships fall apart, and hearts get broken. And it makes aDirada-Do sad.

So it’s important to be as honest as possible with the people you trust, so that your relationships grow strong. And when you say something false on accident, it’s important to admit you were wrong and correct your information.

# Conclusion

A lie is something you say, write, or do with the intention of deceiving other people. It doesn’t if matter what you say is true or not, what matters is if you believe it’s true and are trying to convey truth to the best of your ability.
