# Topic

So what is an identity? What does it mean to identify as something? Do I have to be an artist to identify as one?

Your identity is part of who you are. It’s something you would willingly label yourself as. When someone asks you, “What are you?” the answer to that question is your identity. For example, if you say “an artist”, then “artist” is part of your identity. If you say “American”, then that means that being a resident of the United States is part of your identity.

An identity can be more than one thing. For example, I identity as both a cyclist and a furry. They’re both part of my identity.

I’m also a man, as in human male, I fit the definition of “man”. But is that part of my identity? For me personally, it is not. For some people, it is, and that’s ok. But just because you fit the definition of something, does that mean it has to be part of your identity? No.

For example, you know the word “barbarian”, right? Do you know the original definition? Originally it just meant someone who spoke a language other than Greek[^1]. So do you speak Greek? If not, then by definition, you are a barbarian. But do you identify as a barbarian? Probably not. Should you? Well if you say you have to identify as something you fit the definition for, then yes. And that also means you have to identify as other words you fit the definition for, including, for example, things like “infidel”, “atheist”, “normie”, and so on. And there’s lots more words that you fit the definition for, like “primate”, “animal”, “mammal”, and so on. Do you have to include all those words too in your identity? Of course not, that would be absurd. Your identity is about what makes you personally you, regardless of what words you fit the definition for.

[^1]: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbarian>

So now we know that our identity does not have to include everything we are. But can it include things we’re not?

Me personally, I identify as a deer. Yes, the animal. And yes, I’m aware that in real life, I’m not physically a deer. But that doesn’t matter. Because it’s still part of my identity.

This question came up in the hayday of the Brony fandom. People asked what is a Brony? And the obvious answer was “someone who is a fan of the show, My Little Pony: Friendship is Magic”. But what does that mean? Does it just mean people who engage with the fandom in meetups and online forums? Or can it just be someone who’s watched the show once?

If you say someone has to go to a brony convention at least once a year to be a Brony, then you’re automatically excluding people in the community. If you say you have to have seen every episode, then you’re excluding people who stopped watching when Starlight Glimmer became part of the main cast. Where do you draw the line? Who gets to decide? Whoever does draw the line, that person is gatekeeping.

And what about the opposite, can you force the label “brony” on someone? Can you force someone who’s watched the entire show front to back one time to be a brony? No.

The easy answer is to say that whoever self-identifies as a brony is one. That’s not to say that the definition of a brony is “someone who identifies as one”. The definition of a brony is still the same, except that you don’t determine who is and who isn’t one by just looking at someone. Only the person themselves can decide if they’re one or not. They get to choose if they’re a fan of the show enough to be a brony.

So, someone who has only watched one episode could self-identify as a brony, and someone who’s seen every episode and goes to conventions all the time could decide to not self-identify as a brony. It’s all up to the person whether they are one or not, whether it’s part of their identity or not.

So when I say I identify as a deer, I know I don’t actually fit the definition of one. But it still has meaning, that I have that as part of my identity. It’s more than just my favorite animal, it’s part of my person, part of who I am. That’s what an identity is.

One relevant application to this is trans people. A trans person is someone who is born as one sex, but identifies as another. For example, a man who identifies as a woman.

People are quick to point out that a man isn’t a woman. And they are right, but they’re missing the point. It doesn’t matter if the woman is actually a man “pretending” to be a woman, they’re a woman because they self-identify as one. And to treat them as a man, intentionally using the wrong pronouns, and denying them access to places women go, is wrong. Yes, intentionally breaking someone’s self-identity, is wrong. It’s like telling them that they’re wrong for choosing what they self-identify as. It’s saying they’re wrong for being who they are, that their existence, their very sense of self, is wrong. It’s wrong to tell someone their identity is wrong. Only they can decide for themselves what they identify as, what their identity is.

And that’s not to mention, identities and realities can change. Someone could identify as something before they fit the definition of it, and someone can fit the definition of something before it becomes part of their definition. Going back to the trans example, a man can identify as a woman and then later become a woman. And going back to the artist example, someone can be doing art for a long time before officially self-identifying as an artist. Imposter syndrome is real, yall.

And identities, can they change? Yes. People change their identities all the time. For example, when you were a kid, what did you want to be when you grew up? For me, I wanted to be a paleontologist, because I was super into dinosaurs back then. So you could say that “paleontologist” used to be part of my identity. But when I grew up, I wanted to do computer stuff, so I became a software developer, and “software developer” became part of my identity.

Another example is religion. A lot of atheists out there grew up in the Christian faith. I was one of them. I used to identify as a Christian and was very devout in my faith. Now, I no longer identify as a Christian, despite the fact I still have some of my old habits from those days. Now, I fit the definition of an atheist, but it’s not part of my identity. Why? Because I personally don’t define myself by what I’m not. It’s ok if you choose to, tho.

So, a person can change their identity. Most people have changed their identity at least once or twice in their life.

But changing identity can be hard. For me, the most difficult transition was when I lost my faith. My identity was so wrapped up in being a Christian, that it was very painful to lose it. Losing part of your identity is like losing a part of yourself, and it hurts.

For example, in Puss in Boots: The Last Wish, (minor spoilers by the way), Puss in Boots was going to get married to Kitty Softpaws, but the thing was, that part of Puss in Boots’ identity was as the lone folk hero, and getting married contradicted the “lone” part. So instead of getting married, he chose his own ego, he chose to preserve his own sense of self, and he skipped the wedding. Through the course of the movie, his sense of self got shattered, and he built up a new sense of self with his friends, Kitty Softpaws and Perrito. Now, getting together with Kitty Softpaws was easier for him, because he no longer had “lone folk hero” as part of his identity.

“Ego” is another word for sense of self, for identity. People usually use this word when referring to a broken ego, or someone who has too much ego, etc. Usually it’s used to describe someone who has part of their identity wrapped up in being the best at something or being better than others. But in reality, everyone has an ego, a self of sense, that they don’t want to break, and they don’t want others to break either. And as a reminder, intentionally breaking someone’s ego, is rude, and wrong.

If you walk up to me and tell me “youre not a deer”, youre being very rude. There’s no other reason to tell that to me, other than to break my ego. It’s not constructive and it doesn’t build trust. It’s polite and respectful to honor other people’s sense of self.

# Conclusion

What is an identity? It’s whatever you choose to self-identify as.

Do you have to self-identify as something just because you are that something? No.

Can you self-identify as something you’re not? Yes.

Can your identity change? Yes.

Should you respect what other people self-identify as? Yes.
